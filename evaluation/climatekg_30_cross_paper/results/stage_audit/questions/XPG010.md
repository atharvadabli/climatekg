## Generated answer

Higher daytime low-cloud occurrence over forests is driven by forest surface properties—specifically lower albedo and higher surface roughness—which enhance the turbulent exchange of heat and moisture. This promotes stronger thermal convection and moistening of the atmospheric boundary layer, leading to increased cloud frequency. Observations from summer periods show that broadleaf forests exhibit significantly higher daytime cloud frequencies compared to surrounding agricultural land, with clouds developing earlier in the morning and persisting longer into the evening. [# Observational evidence for cloud cover enhancement over western European forests (2016), pp. 4,6, Claim P000014_CL004; # Observational evidence for cloud cover enhancement over western European forests (2016), pp. 2, Claim P000014_CL005; # Observational evidence for cloud cover enhancement over western European forests (2016), pp. 2, Claim P000014_CL001; # Observational evidence for cloud cover enhancement over western European forests (2016), pp. 4, Claim P000014_CL006; # Observational evidence for cloud cover enhancement over western European forests (2016), pp. 4, Claim P000014_CL002]

The connected mechanism links forest canopy characteristics to boundary-layer dynamics. Lower albedo and higher aerodynamic roughness facilitate more efficient exchange of sensible heat and latent moisture into the atmosphere. This results in enhanced thermal convection and greater moistening of the atmospheric boundary layer, which advances the onset and extends the duration of convective activity favorable for low-cloud formation. [# Observational evidence for cloud cover enhancement over western European forests (2016), pp. 4,6, Claim P000014_CL004]

The documented mechanisms and cloud frequency increases apply specifically to summer daytime conditions over broadleaf forests (e.g., Sologne and Landes regions) relative to adjacent agricultural land. The evidence does not support spatial transfer to other forest types, latitudes, or seasons without additional local environmental data. [# Observational evidence for cloud cover enhancement over western European forests (2016), pp. 2, Claim P000014_CL005; # Observational evidence for cloud cover enhancement over western European forests (2016), pp. 2, Claim P000014_CL001]

While the cloud-enhancing mechanism relies partly on heat absorption from lower albedo, other findings indicate that forests do not universally warm the surface during the day. Globally, daytime land surface temperatures over forests are often cooler than open land because high evapotranspiration offsets the warming from low albedo. Conversely, in high-latitude boreal regions, negligible evapotranspiration allows the low-albedo warming effect to dominate. These temperature contrasts highlight that the boundary-layer moistening and convection driving cloud formation depend on a balance of surface energy fluxes that varies by climate zone. [# Local cooling and warming effects of forests based on satellite observations (2014), pp. 2,3, Claim P000095_CL001; # Local cooling and warming effects of forests based on satellite observations (2014), pp. 2,3, Claim P000095_CL002]
