## Generated answer

Irrigation reduces dry-bulb temperature extremes by shifting the surface energy balance: it increases latent heat flux through evapotranspiration while decreasing sensible heat flux, leading to evaporative cooling that lowers land surface and 2-meter air temperatures. This mechanism is particularly effective in regions with high evaporative demand and low background evaporative fractions, such as India, the Middle East, and North America, where significant cooling of annual maximum daytime temperatures is observed. [# Moist heat stress extremes in India enhanced by irrigation (1970), pp. 4, Claim P000039_CL001; # Moist heat stress extremes in India enhanced by irrigation (1970), pp. 4, Claim P000039_CL002; # Moist heat stress extremes in India enhanced by irrigation (1970), pp. 4, Claim P000039_CL003; # Moist heat stress extremes in India enhanced by irrigation (1970), pp. 4, Claim P000039_CL004; # Irrigation induced surface cooling in the context of modern and increased greenhouse gas forcing (2010), pp. 5, Claim P000037_CL001; # Journal of Geophysical Research: Atmospheres (2017), pp. 12, Claim P000004_CL004]

Conversely, irrigation increases moist-heat risk by raising specific and relative humidity. The reduction in sensible heat flux causes surface cooling and subsidence, which collapses the planetary boundary layer height. This shallower boundary layer concentrates moisture near the surface, enhancing low-level moist enthalpy and leading to an increase in extreme wet-bulb temperatures. [# Moist heat stress extremes in India enhanced by irrigation (1970), pp. 5, Claim P000039_CL006; # Moist heat stress extremes in India enhanced by irrigation (1970), pp. 5, Claim P000039_CL007; # Moist heat stress extremes in India enhanced by irrigation (1970), pp. 5, Claim P000039_CL005; # Moist heat stress extremes in India enhanced by irrigation (1970), pp. 5, Claim P000039_CL011]

This trade-off—reduced dry heat stress but increased moist-heat risk—is explicitly observed in WRF model simulations over the Indo-Gangetic Plain during summer. In this setting, irrigation decreases extreme dry heat stress (95th percentile of 2-meter air temperature) and extreme Heat Index because cooling dominates, yet it simultaneously increases extreme wet-bulb temperatures due to moisture concentration in a shallower boundary layer. [# Moist heat stress extremes in India enhanced by irrigation (1970), pp. 5, Claim P000039_CL009; # Moist heat stress extremes in India enhanced by irrigation (1970), pp. 5, Claim P000039_CL010; # Moist heat stress extremes in India enhanced by irrigation (1970), pp. 5, Claim P000039_CL011]

The magnitude of cooling varies by regional climate; for instance, in energy-limited regimes with high background evaporative fractions like China and Eastern Asia, the temperature reduction from irrigation is modest or negligible. Additionally, while extreme wet-bulb temperatures increase, some studies note that the impact on average maximum wet-bulb temperatures can be larger than on extremes, particularly during drier early summer months. [# Irrigation induced surface cooling in the context of modern and increased greenhouse gas forcing (2010), pp. 5, Claim P000037_CL006; North China Plain threatened by deadly heatwaves due to climate change and irrigation (1951), pp. 2,4, Claim P000038_CL003]
