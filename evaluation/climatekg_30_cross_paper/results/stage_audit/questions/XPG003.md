Why does drought remove the summer-extreme cooling associated with rainfed crop intensification but not necessarily the cooling associated with irrigation, and how does background evaporative fraction modify irrigation sensitivity?

Rainfed intensification cools through evapotranspiration only while soil moisture is available, so its cooling disappears in drought. Irrigation supplies water and can preserve latent cooling. ## Generated answer

Drought removes the summer-extreme cooling associated with rainfed crop intensification because inadequate soil moisture eliminates the ability to achieve greater rates of evapotranspiration, causing baseline cooler temperatures to revert to historically hot extremes. In contrast, irrigation-induced cooling persists during drought conditions because supplemental water maintains evapotranspiration capacity and reduces sensible heat flux, thereby sustaining lower surface and air temperatures even when background soil moisture is low. [# Cooling of US Midwest summer temperature extremes from cropland intensification (1910), pp. 5, Claim P000007_CL010; # Cooling of US Midwest summer temperature extremes from cropland intensification (1910), pp. 5, Claim P000007_CL011; # Cooling of US Midwest summer temperature extremes from cropland intensification (1910), pp. 4,5, Claim P000007_CL008; # Journal of Geophysical Research: Atmospheres (2017), pp. 8,12, Claim P000004_CL003]

The sensitivity of irrigation-induced cooling is modified by the background evaporative fraction such that regions with low evaporative fractions (below 0.2–0.4), which are moisture-limited, exhibit larger temperature responses to added water compared to energy-limited regions. [# Irrigation induced surface cooling in the context of modern and increased greenhouse gas forcing (2010), pp. 4, Claim P000037_CL004]
